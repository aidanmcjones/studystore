The exam gives you a periodic table, but not the trends, so the useful skill is predicting direction. Almost every trend comes from two forces: effective nuclear charge (how strongly the nucleus pulls the outer electrons) and distance (how many shells lie between). Learn those two and the trends follow.

The two drivers

  • Across a period (left to right): protons are added, but electrons enter the same shell, so shielding barely changes. The pull on outer electrons grows and the atom shrinks.
  • Down a group: a new shell is added, so the outer electrons sit farther away and are shielded by the inner shells. The atom grows and the pull weakens.

The trends

PropertyAcross a periodDown a group
Atomic radiusDecreasesIncreases
Ionization energyIncreasesDecreases
Electron affinity (magnitude)Generally increasesGenerally decreases
ElectronegativityIncreasesDecreases
Metallic characterDecreasesIncreases

Fluorine is the most electronegative element and cesium and francium are among the least. The noble gases are usually left out of electronegativity discussions because they rarely form bonds.

Reasons worth memorizing

  • Ionization energy is the energy to remove an outer electron. A small atom with a strong pull holds on tightly, so fluorine and helium have high values and cesium has a low one.
  • Electron affinity is the energy released when an atom gains an electron. Halogens release the most; noble gases and the alkaline earths release little or none.
  • Electronegativity is the pull on shared electrons in a bond. It is the basis for deciding whether a bond is polar covalent or ionic.
  • Atomic radius for cations is smaller than the neutral atom (fewer electrons, same protons); anions are larger.

Exceptions the test likes

  • Successive ionization energies: each is larger than the last, and there is a large jump when you start removing core electrons (for example, from the second to the third ionization energy of magnesium).
  • Group 2 to group 13: ionization energy dips from beryllium to boron because the new electron is in a higher-energy p orbital.
  • Group 15 to group 16: ionization energy dips from nitrogen to oxygen because pairing electrons in one p orbital adds repulsion.
  • Isoelectronic species: for ions with the same electron count (such as O2-, F-, Na+, Mg2+), the one with more protons is smaller.

Using trends in a passage

  1. Locate the elements in the periodic table. Same period or same group?
  2. Pick the property being asked about and apply the table above.
  3. For cations and anions, compare charge, then size.
  4. Reconcile with the passage data; if a table of values contradicts the trend, check for one of the exceptions.

Common traps

  • Saying radius decreases down a group. It increases.
  • Forgetting that cations are smaller. Losing an electron shell or reducing repulsion shrinks the ion.
  • Treating electron affinity and electronegativity as the same. One is an energy of a gaseous atom, the other a bonding tendency.
  • Applying trends to the noble gases blindly. Their electron affinities and electronegativities are special cases.

One-minute recap

  • Across a period, the pull strengthens, so radius falls and ionization energy and electronegativity rise.
  • Down a group, shells are added, so radius rises and ionization energy and electronegativity fall.
  • Know the dips at B and O, and the jump after removing valence electrons.

Check yourself: 5-question mini-quiz

Try each question first, then click it to reveal the answer.

1. What happens to atomic radius across a period?

It decreases, because effective nuclear charge increases with the same shell.

2. Which element is the most electronegative?

Fluorine.

3. Why does ionization energy dip from nitrogen to oxygen?

Pairing electrons in one p orbital adds repulsion, making one easier to remove.

4. Which is smaller, Na+ or Na?

Na+, since it has lost its outer shell.

5. Among O2-, F-, Na+ and Mg2+, which is smallest?

Mg2+, which has the most protons for the same number of electrons.
