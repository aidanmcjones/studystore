Both are attribution biases, and both can show up in the same story, which is why they get confused. Self-serving bias depends on the outcome: you credit yourself for success and blame the situation for failure. Actor-observer bias depends on the perspective: you explain your own behavior by the situation, but explain other people's behavior by their disposition.

Ask two questions. Was the outcome good or bad? Whose behavior is being explained?

Side by side

FeatureSelf-serving biasActor-observer bias
What drives the patternSuccess vs failureActor vs observer
Whose behaviorYour ownYour own and others' (the contrast is the point)
SuccessInternal: "I'm skilled"No fixed rule; depends on who is explaining
FailureExternal: "the test was unfair"Actor says situation; observer says disposition
MotiveProtects self-esteemDifferences in information and focus

Examples

  • Self-serving: you ace an exam and say you studied hard. You fail the next one and say the questions were tricky. Same person, same behavior type, different explanation because the outcome flipped.
  • Actor-observer: you cut someone off in traffic because you were late (situation). When someone else cuts you off, you call them reckless (disposition). The outcome is the same in both cases; only the viewpoint changed.

Why actor-observer happens

The actor sees the situation, because the situation is what they are looking at, and knows their own history of acting differently in different settings. The observer sees the person, since the person is the most noticeable thing in view, and has little information about the situation. That is the same logic behind the fundamental attribution error, applied to the self versus others.

How it relates to the fundamental attribution error

The fundamental attribution error is the observer half of actor-observer bias: over-crediting disposition when explaining someone else. Actor-observer bias adds the actor half and the contrast. See the FAE vs actor-observer note and the self-serving bias vs FAE note.

Telling them apart in a passage

  1. If the stem stresses success or failure and the person is explaining themselves, pick self-serving bias.
  2. If the stem contrasts how someone explains their own behavior with how they explain a friend's or stranger's, pick actor-observer bias.
  3. If it only describes blaming another person's character, pick the fundamental attribution error.

Common traps

  • Using "self-serving" for any self-explanation. It requires the explanation to track the outcome.
  • Using "actor-observer" when only one person is described. It needs the self-versus-other contrast.
  • Reading self-serving bias as always internal. Internal for success, external for failure.
  • Treating the FAE as a different thing from the observer side. It is the same tendency.

One-minute recap

Self-serving bias: flattering explanations for your own results. Actor-observer bias: situation for me, disposition for you. The first turns on outcome, the second on perspective.

Check yourself: 5-question mini-quiz

Try each question first, then click it to reveal the answer.

1. A student blames a bad grade on an unfair exam but credits a good grade to hard work. Which bias is this?

Self-serving bias. The explanation changes with the outcome and protects self-esteem.

2. You say you were late because of traffic, but you say a coworker was late because he is disorganized. Which bias is this?

Actor-observer bias. Situation for your own behavior, disposition for someone else's.

3. What is the key variable in self-serving bias, and what is it in actor-observer bias?

Self-serving bias turns on the outcome (success or failure). Actor-observer bias turns on perspective (actor or observer).

4. Which bias is the observer half of actor-observer bias?

The fundamental attribution error, which over-credits disposition when explaining other people's behavior.

5. A player says his team won because of his skill and lost because of the referee. Which bias applies?

Self-serving bias, since the attribution follows the outcome.
