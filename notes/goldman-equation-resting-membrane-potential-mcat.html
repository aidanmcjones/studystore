The Nernst equation gives the equilibrium potential of one ion. The Goldman equation gives the membrane potential when several ions can cross, weighting each ion by its permeability.

The idea

Vm = 61.5 log [ (PK[K+]out + PNa[Na+]out + PCl[Cl-]in) / (PK[K+]in + PNa[Na+]in + PCl[Cl-]out) ] mV at 37 C.

Anions have inside and outside swapped. You do not need to memorize it. You need the logic: the membrane potential is pulled toward the equilibrium potential of the most permeable ion.

Typical equilibrium potentials

IonE (mV)
K+about -90
Na+about +60
Cl-about -70

At rest the neuron is far more permeable to K+ (leak channels) than Na+, so Vm sits near -70 mV, close to EK but not equal to it.

Why it is not exactly EK

A small Na+ leak pulls Vm up from -90 mV. That is the whole difference.

Role of Na+/K+ ATPase

It pumps 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in per ATP. It contributes a few mV of negative charge, but its main job is to maintain the gradients that the leak channels use. Block it and the gradients slowly run down, not instantly.

What shifts Vm

  • Open voltage-gated Na+ channels: PNa rises, Vm moves toward +60 (depolarization).
  • Open K+ channels more: toward -90 (hyperpolarization).
  • Raise extracellular K+: EK becomes less negative, so the cell depolarizes.

Common traps

  • Saying Vm equals EK. It is close, not equal.
  • Using Nernst for a multi-ion membrane. Nernst needs a membrane permeable to one ion.
  • Blaming the pump alone for the resting potential. Gradients plus K+ permeability set it.

One-minute recap

  • Goldman: permeability-weighted version of Nernst.
  • Resting potential near -70 mV because K+ permeability dominates.
  • Raising extracellular K+ depolarizes the cell.

Check yourself: 5-question mini-quiz

Try each question first, then click it to reveal the answer.

1. How does the Goldman equation differ from Nernst?

It includes several ions, each weighted by its permeability.

2. Why is the resting potential near -70 mV?

The membrane is mostly permeable to K+, which has E near -90 mV, with a small Na+ leak.

3. What happens if extracellular K+ rises?

E_K becomes less negative and the cell depolarizes.

4. Which ion's equilibrium potential does the membrane move toward when P_Na rises?

Sodium, about +60 mV.

5. What is the stoichiometry of the Na+/K+ ATPase?

3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in per ATP.
