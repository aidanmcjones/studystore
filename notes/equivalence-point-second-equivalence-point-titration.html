Equivalence Point vs End Point, and the Second Equivalence Point

By StudyStore · Published 2026-10-26 · 4 min read · 963 words

The equivalence point is the moment in a titration when the moles of titrant added exactly equal the moles of analyte that can react with it. A polyprotic acid has one equivalence point per ionizable proton, so a diprotic acid has a first and a second equivalence point, and the second falls at exactly twice the titrant volume of the first. The end point is different: it is where the indicator changes color, which is chosen to land close to the equivalence point.

Equivalence point vs end point

Equivalence pointEnd point
DefinitionMoles of titrant = moles of analyte (by the reaction stoichiometry)The observed signal, such as an indicator color change
How you find itCalculated, or read from the steepest part of a pH curveSeen in the flask

Ideal relationshipThe targetWithin a drop or two of the equivalence point

The gap between the two is the titration error. A good indicator has a color-change range that falls on the steep part of the curve, so the error is a fraction of a drop.

Finding the equivalence point

For a monoprotic acid and a monobasic titrant, the equivalence point occurs when Macid × Vacid = Mbase × Vbase. If 25.0 mL of 0.100 M acid is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH, equivalence is at 25.0 mL. On a curve it is the midpoint of the steepest vertical section. Its pH depends on the species left in the flask: 7 for a strong acid with a strong base, above 7 for a weak acid with a strong base (the conjugate base makes OH-), and below 7 for a weak base with a strong acid.

The second equivalence point of a polyprotic acid

Each proton takes one equivalent of base. For a diprotic acid H2A:

  1. First equivalence point: H2A has all become HA-. The volume is V1 = moles of H2A / Mbase.
  2. Second equivalence point: HA- has all become A2-. The volume is V2 = 2 × V1.
  3. First half-equivalence point (V1/2): [H2A] = [HA-], so pH = pKa1.
  4. Second half-equivalence point (1.5 × V1): [HA-] = [A2-], so pH = pKa2.
  5. At the first equivalence point, the species is amphoteric, and pH is about (pKa1 + pKa2) / 2. For an amino acid with no ionizable side chain, this is the isoelectric point.
  6. At the second equivalence point, the species is the fully deprotonated base A2-, so the pH is basic and set by its hydrolysis.

Worked example: glycine

Titrate 25.0 mL of 0.100 M fully protonated glycine (pKa1 = 2.34, pKa2 = 9.60) with 0.100 M NaOH.

  • Moles of glycine: 0.100 M × 0.0250 L = 2.50 mmol. Each equivalence point needs 2.50 mmol of NaOH per proton.
  • First equivalence point: 25.0 mL. pH is about (2.34 + 9.60) / 2 = 5.97.
  • Second equivalence point: 50.0 mL.
  • Half-equivalence points: pH = 2.34 at 12.5 mL and pH = 9.60 at 37.5 mL.

At 50.0 mL, the total volume is 75.0 mL, so [glycinate] = 2.50 mmol / 75.0 mL = 0.0333 M. Glycinate is a weak base with Kb = 10-14 / 10-9.60 = 10-4.40 = 4.0 × 10-5. Then [OH-] = √(Kb × C) = √(4.0 × 10-5 × 0.0333) = 1.15 × 10-3 M, so pOH = 2.94 and pH = 11.06.

What "trial 1" means in a lab titration

In a lab, trial 1 is usually a rough run. You add titrant fairly quickly to find approximately where the color changes, so you know when to slow down. Later trials add titrant dropwise near that volume. The accepted result is the average of trials that agree closely (commonly within 0.1 mL), and the rough trial 1 is often left out of the average. A trial that overshoots the end point is discarded, not corrected.

Common traps

  • Assuming the second equivalence point is at the same pH as the first. It is at twice the volume, and a very different pH.
  • Using the wrong pKa at a half-equivalence point. The first half-equivalence point gives pKa1 and the second gives pKa2.
  • Forgetting dilution at the second equivalence point. Total volume has doubled, which matters when you compute a concentration.
  • Treating the end point as exact. It is the indicator's color change, and only approximates the equivalence point.
  • Expecting two clear jumps when the pKa values are close. The two steps blur together unless the values differ by several units.

One-minute recap

Equivalence is stoichiometric and calculated, and the end point is observed. A diprotic acid has equivalence points at V1 and 2V1, with pKa1 at V1/2 and pKa2 at 1.5V1. The first equivalence pH is the average of the two pKa values, and the second is set by the fully deprotonated base.

Check yourself: 5-question mini-quiz

Try each question first, then click it to reveal the answer.

1. What is the difference between the equivalence point and the end point of a titration?

The equivalence point is where moles of titrant equal moles of analyte by stoichiometry. The end point is where the indicator visibly changes color. A well-chosen indicator makes them nearly identical.

2. A diprotic acid reaches its first equivalence point at 20.0 mL of NaOH. Where is the second equivalence point?

At 40.0 mL. Removing the second proton takes another full equivalent of base.

3. For the same diprotic acid, at what volume is pH equal to pKa2?

At 30.0 mL, the midpoint between the first and second equivalence points, where [HA-] = [A2-].

4. What is the approximate pH at the first equivalence point of a diprotic acid with pKa1 = 4.0 and pKa2 = 8.0?

About 6.0, the average of the two pKa values, because the amphoteric species HA- dominates.

5. Why is the pH above 7 at the second equivalence point?

All the acid has become the fully deprotonated conjugate base A2-, which is a weak base and reacts with water to make hydroxide.
