Infrared (IR) spectroscopy detects bond vibrations. A bond absorbs infrared light when the light's frequency matches the bond's stretching frequency and the vibration changes the molecule's dipole moment. The MCAT does not ask you to memorize a full correlation chart. It asks you to recognize a handful of regions, mostly O-H and N-H above 3000 cm-1 and C=O near 1700 cm-1, and to reason about why they look the way they do.

What sets the wavenumber

IR spectra are plotted in wavenumber (cm-1), which is proportional to frequency and therefore to energy. Stronger, stiffer bonds and lighter atoms vibrate at higher wavenumber:

  • Bond strength. Triple bonds absorb above double bonds, which absorb above single bonds: C≡C and C≡N near 2200, C=C and C=O near 1600 to 1750, C-O near 1000 to 1300.
  • Atomic mass. Bonds to hydrogen are the lightest, so O-H, N-H and C-H stretches all sit high, above about 2800 cm-1.

Why O-H is broad and C=O is sharp

Hydroxyl groups hydrogen bond with each other, and each molecule sits in a slightly different hydrogen bonding environment. That spread of bond strengths smears the absorption into a wide band. A carbonyl does not hydrogen bond with itself, and its large dipole change gives an intense, narrow peak. A dilute alcohol in a nonpolar solvent loses much of its hydrogen bonding, so its O-H band becomes sharper and shifts to higher wavenumber.

Telling carbonyls apart

All carbonyls absorb near 1700, so the other peaks decide which one you have:

CompoundC=O positionExtra clue
Ketoneabout 1715No O-H, no aldehyde C-H
Aldehydeabout 1725Two weak C-H peaks near 2720 and 2820
Carboxylic acidabout 1710Very broad O-H from 2500 to 3300
Esterabout 1735 to 1745Strong C-O near 1200, no O-H
Amideabout 1650 to 1690N-H near 3300
Conjugated carbonyllowers by 20 to 30Resonance weakens the C=O bond

Esters absorb highest because the alkoxy oxygen is inductively withdrawing, and amides absorb lowest because nitrogen donates strongly by resonance into the carbonyl, giving it more single-bond character. The same resonance logic ranks acid derivative reactivity.

Worked example 1: oxidation of an alcohol

A student oxidizes 2-butanol and wants to know if the reaction worked. The starting material shows a broad band at 3350 cm-1. The product spectrum has no band above 3000 cm-1 except C-H, and a new sharp peak at 1715 cm-1. The O-H is gone and a ketone C=O appeared, so 2-butanone formed.

Worked example 2: acid or alcohol with a carbonyl

An unknown has a very broad absorption from 2500 to 3300 cm-1 and a strong peak at 1710 cm-1. An alcohol plus a ketone could give both a broad O-H and a C=O, but the O-H of an alcohol is centered near 3350 and does not stretch down to 2500. The extremely wide band that swallows the C-H region points to a carboxylic acid.

Worked example 3: amide versus amine

A compound shows two peaks near 3350 and 3280 cm-1 and a strong peak at 1660 cm-1. Two N-H peaks mean an NH2 group, and 1660 is low for a carbonyl, which fits an amide. A primary amine alone would have no peak near 1660.

What IR cannot tell you

IR identifies functional groups but not how many hydrogens or carbons you have or how they connect. Use <a href="how-to-read-ir-and-nmr-spectra">NMR with IR</a> to build the skeleton. The region below about 1500 cm-1, the fingerprint region, is unique to each molecule but is rarely used for MCAT reasoning.

Common traps

  • Calling every peak near 3000 an O-H. C-H stretches live at 2850 to 3000, with sp2 C-H just above 3000.
  • Forgetting the shape. Broad usually means O-H, sharp and strong usually means C=O.
  • Ignoring symmetry. A bond with no change in dipole moment, such as the C≡C of a symmetric alkyne, is IR inactive or very weak.
  • Ranking carbonyls backward. Resonance donation lowers the C=O wavenumber, so amides sit below ketones, and esters sit above.
  • Reading position without intensity. A weak peak near 1650 can be C=C, while a strong one is C=O.

One-minute recap

Above 3000 cm-1 look for O-H (broad) and N-H (sharper, two peaks for NH2). Near 1700 look for the strong, sharp C=O, and use the other peaks to decide among ketone, aldehyde, acid, ester and amide. Triple bonds sit near 2200, C=C near 1650, and C-O in the 1000 to 1300 range. Hydrogen bonding broadens O-H, and resonance lowers C=O.

Check yourself: 5-question mini-quiz

Try each question first, then click it to reveal the answer.

1. A spectrum has a strong, sharp peak at 1715 cm-1 and no absorption above 3000 cm-1 except ordinary C-H. Which functional group is most likely present?

A ketone. The sharp C=O near 1715 with no O-H or N-H band rules out acids, alcohols and amides.

2. Why is the O-H stretch of an alcohol broad while the C=O stretch is sharp?

Hydrogen bonding puts O-H bonds in many slightly different environments, which spreads the absorption. C=O does not hydrogen bond with itself and gives a narrow, intense peak.

3. A compound shows a very broad band from 2500 to 3300 cm-1 and a peak at 1710 cm-1. What is it?

A carboxylic acid. The extremely broad O-H band overlapping the C-H region plus a carbonyl is the acid signature.

4. Which absorbs at the higher wavenumber, an ester C=O or an amide C=O, and why?

The ester. Nitrogen donates by resonance into an amide carbonyl, weakening the C=O bond and lowering its wavenumber, while the ester oxygen donates less.

5. An alcohol is oxidized to an aldehyde. What change would you expect in the IR spectrum?

The broad O-H band near 3350 disappears, a strong C=O appears near 1725, and two weak aldehyde C-H peaks appear near 2720 and 2820.
