How are wave speed, frequency, and wavelength related?

v = fλ. In a given medium the speed is fixed, so a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength.

Front · Cloze

Frequency is measured in [...], which equals [...].

Back

Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz), which equals cycles per second (s<sup>-1</sup>).

Q1A wave on a string has f = 20 Hz and λ = 0.50 m. The tension is quadrupled while the source still vibrates at 20 Hz. The new wavelength is:

B. Speed is √(T/μ), so quadrupling T doubles v from 10 to 20 m/s. Frequency is fixed by the source at 20 Hz, so λ = v/f = 1.0 m. 0.25 m would result from a halved speed. 0.50 m ignores the speed change, and 2.0 m would need v to quadruple.

Q2A sonar pulse in seawater (v = 1500 m/s) returns from the bottom 0.20 s after it is sent. The depth is:

B. The pulse goes down and back, so d = vt/2 = 1500 × 0.20/2 = 150 m. 300 m forgets the factor of two. 75 m halves it twice, and 7500 m divides speed by time instead of multiplying.

Q3An ultrasound echo returns 130 μs after the pulse is sent. In tissue (v = 1540 m/s) the reflector is at a depth of:

C. d = vt/2 = 1540 × 130 × 10<sup>-6</sup>/2 = 0.10 m. 20 cm omits the half. 5.0 cm is the 65 μs result, and 1.0 cm would be an order of magnitude off.

Q4White light enters a glass prism. Compared with red light, violet light in the glass has:

A. n is larger for violet, so v = c/n is lower, and the greater index also bends it more. A higher speed would go with the red light. A lower speed cannot come with a smaller deviation, and the speeds differ, which is why dispersion occurs.

Q5A swimmer opening their eyes underwater sees a blurry image mainly because:

D. The cornea (n about 1.38) normally refracts light strongly at the air interface. Against water (n = 1.33) there is almost no index change, so most of the power is lost and the image falls far behind the retina. The lens still works. Water does not absorb enough light over a short path to cause blur, and the retina is unaffected.

Sample questions

5 real questions from this pack, with answers

Q1 A wave on a string has f = 20 Hz and λ = 0.50 m. The tension is quadrupled while the source still vibrates at 20 Hz. The new wavelength is:

  • A. 0.25 m
  • B. 1.0 m
  • C. 0.50 m
  • D. 2.0 m

Answer: B. Speed is √(T/μ), so quadrupling T doubles v from 10 to 20 m/s. Frequency is fixed by the source at 20 Hz, so λ = v/f = 1.0 m. 0.25 m would result from a halved speed. 0.50 m ignores the speed change, and 2.0 m would need v to quadruple.

Q2 A sonar pulse in seawater (v = 1500 m/s) returns from the bottom 0.20 s after it is sent. The depth is:

  • A. 300 m
  • B. 150 m
  • C. 75 m
  • D. 7500 m

Answer: B. The pulse goes down and back, so d = vt/2 = 1500 × 0.20/2 = 150 m. 300 m forgets the factor of two. 75 m halves it twice, and 7500 m divides speed by time instead of multiplying.

Q3 An ultrasound echo returns 130 μs after the pulse is sent. In tissue (v = 1540 m/s) the reflector is at a depth of:

  • A. 20 cm
  • B. 5.0 cm
  • C. 10 cm
  • D. 1.0 cm

Answer: C. d = vt/2 = 1540 × 130 × 10<sup>-6</sup>/2 = 0.10 m. 20 cm omits the half. 5.0 cm is the 65 μs result, and 1.0 cm would be an order of magnitude off.

Q4 White light enters a glass prism. Compared with red light, violet light in the glass has:

  • A. A lower speed and a larger deviation
  • B. A higher speed and a larger deviation
  • C. A lower speed and a smaller deviation
  • D. The same speed and a larger deviation

Answer: A. n is larger for violet, so v = c/n is lower, and the greater index also bends it more. A higher speed would go with the red light. A lower speed cannot come with a smaller deviation, and the speeds differ, which is why dispersion occurs.

Q5 A swimmer opening their eyes underwater sees a blurry image mainly because:

  • A. The lens of the eye stops accommodating
  • B. Water absorbs visible light strongly
  • C. The retina becomes insensitive in water
  • D. The cornea loses most of its refractive power against water

Answer: D. The cornea (n about 1.38) normally refracts light strongly at the air interface. Against water (n = 1.33) there is almost no index change, so most of the power is lost and the image falls far behind the retina. The lens still works. Water does not absorb enough light over a short path to cause blur, and the retina is unaffected.
