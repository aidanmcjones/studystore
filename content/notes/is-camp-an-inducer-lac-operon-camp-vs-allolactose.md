**No. cAMP is not the inducer of the lac operon.** The inducer is **allolactose** (an isomer of lactose made from it). cAMP is the signal that tells the cell glucose is low, and it works through a different protein.

## Two molecules, two jobs

| | Allolactose | cAMP |
|---|---|---|
| Role | **Inducer** | Activating signal (positive control) |
| Binds | The **lac repressor** (LacI) | **CAP** (catabolite activator protein) |
| Effect of binding | Repressor leaves the operator, so RNA polymerase can pass | CAP binds the promoter region and helps RNA polymerase bind |
| Present when | Lactose is present | Glucose is low |

Allolactose **removes a block**. cAMP-CAP **adds a boost**.

## The four cases

| Glucose | Lactose | Repressor | cAMP-CAP | Expression |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High | Absent | On operator | Low | None |
| High | Present | Off operator | Low | Low |
| Low | Absent | On operator | High | None |
| Low | Present | Off operator | High | **High** |

Both conditions must be met for high expression: lactose present (repressor off) **and** glucose low (CAP on).

## Why glucose lowers cAMP

High glucose lowers adenylate cyclase activity, so cAMP falls. Without cAMP, CAP cannot bind and transcription stays weak even when the repressor is off. This is **catabolite repression**.

## Common traps

- **Calling cAMP the inducer.** Inducer means it inactivates the repressor. Only allolactose does that.
- **Thinking low glucose alone turns the operon on.** Without lactose the repressor still blocks it.
- **Mixing up the two proteins.** The repressor responds to allolactose; CAP responds to cAMP.

## One-minute recap

- Allolactose = inducer, binds the repressor.
- cAMP binds CAP, a positive regulator.
- High expression needs lactose present and glucose low.
