Infrared (IR) spectroscopy detects bond vibrations. A bond absorbs infrared light when the light's frequency matches the bond's stretching frequency and the vibration changes the molecule's dipole moment. The MCAT does not ask you to memorize a full correlation chart. It asks you to recognize a handful of regions, mostly **O-H and N-H above 3000 cm<sup>-1</sup>** and **C=O near 1700 cm<sup>-1</sup>**, and to reason about why they look the way they do.

## What sets the wavenumber

IR spectra are plotted in wavenumber (cm<sup>-1</sup>), which is proportional to frequency and therefore to energy. Stronger, stiffer bonds and lighter atoms vibrate at higher wavenumber:

- **Bond strength.** Triple bonds absorb above double bonds, which absorb above single bonds: C&equiv;C and C&equiv;N near 2200, C=C and C=O near 1600 to 1750, C-O near 1000 to 1300.
- **Atomic mass.** Bonds to hydrogen are the lightest, so O-H, N-H and C-H stretches all sit high, above about 2800 cm<sup>-1</sup>.

## The regions to know

| Bond | Approx. wavenumber (cm<sup>-1</sup>) | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| O-H, alcohol | 3200 to 3600 | Strong and broad |
| O-H, carboxylic acid | 2500 to 3300 | Very broad, overlaps C-H |
| N-H, amine or amide | 3300 to 3500 | Medium, sharper than O-H; primary amines show two peaks |
| C-H, sp<sup>3</sup> | 2850 to 3000 | Medium |
| C-H, aldehyde | about 2720 and 2820 | Two weak peaks |
| C&equiv;N, C&equiv;C | 2100 to 2260 | Weak to medium |
| C=O | 1670 to 1780 | Strong and sharp |
| C=C | 1620 to 1680 | Weak to medium |
| C-O | 1000 to 1300 | Strong |

## Why O-H is broad and C=O is sharp

Hydroxyl groups hydrogen bond with each other, and each molecule sits in a slightly different hydrogen bonding environment. That spread of bond strengths smears the absorption into a wide band. A carbonyl does not hydrogen bond with itself, and its large dipole change gives an intense, narrow peak. A dilute alcohol in a nonpolar solvent loses much of its hydrogen bonding, so its O-H band becomes sharper and shifts to higher wavenumber.

## Telling carbonyls apart

All carbonyls absorb near 1700, so the other peaks decide which one you have:

| Compound | C=O position | Extra clue |
|---|---|---|
| Ketone | about 1715 | No O-H, no aldehyde C-H |
| Aldehyde | about 1725 | Two weak C-H peaks near 2720 and 2820 |
| Carboxylic acid | about 1710 | Very broad O-H from 2500 to 3300 |
| Ester | about 1735 to 1745 | Strong C-O near 1200, no O-H |
| Amide | about 1650 to 1690 | N-H near 3300 |
| Conjugated carbonyl | lowers by 20 to 30 | Resonance weakens the C=O bond |

Esters absorb highest because the alkoxy oxygen is inductively withdrawing, and amides absorb lowest because nitrogen donates strongly by resonance into the carbonyl, giving it more single-bond character. The same resonance logic ranks acid derivative reactivity.

## Worked example 1: oxidation of an alcohol

A student oxidizes 2-butanol and wants to know if the reaction worked. The starting material shows a broad band at 3350 cm<sup>-1</sup>. The product spectrum has no band above 3000 cm<sup>-1</sup> except C-H, and a new sharp peak at 1715 cm<sup>-1</sup>. The O-H is gone and a ketone C=O appeared, so 2-butanone formed.

## Worked example 2: acid or alcohol with a carbonyl

An unknown has a very broad absorption from 2500 to 3300 cm<sup>-1</sup> and a strong peak at 1710 cm<sup>-1</sup>. An alcohol plus a ketone could give both a broad O-H and a C=O, but the O-H of an alcohol is centered near 3350 and does not stretch down to 2500. The extremely wide band that swallows the C-H region points to a **carboxylic acid**.

## Worked example 3: amide versus amine

A compound shows two peaks near 3350 and 3280 cm<sup>-1</sup> and a strong peak at 1660 cm<sup>-1</sup>. Two N-H peaks mean an NH<sub>2</sub> group, and 1660 is low for a carbonyl, which fits an amide. A primary amine alone would have no peak near 1660.

## What IR cannot tell you

IR identifies functional groups but not how many hydrogens or carbons you have or how they connect. Use <a href="how-to-read-ir-and-nmr-spectra.html">NMR with IR</a> to build the skeleton. The region below about 1500 cm<sup>-1</sup>, the fingerprint region, is unique to each molecule but is rarely used for MCAT reasoning.

## Common traps

- **Calling every peak near 3000 an O-H.** C-H stretches live at 2850 to 3000, with sp<sup>2</sup> C-H just above 3000.
- **Forgetting the shape.** Broad usually means O-H, sharp and strong usually means C=O.
- **Ignoring symmetry.** A bond with no change in dipole moment, such as the C&equiv;C of a symmetric alkyne, is IR inactive or very weak.
- **Ranking carbonyls backward.** Resonance donation lowers the C=O wavenumber, so amides sit below ketones, and esters sit above.
- **Reading position without intensity.** A weak peak near 1650 can be C=C, while a strong one is C=O.

## One-minute recap

Above 3000 cm<sup>-1</sup> look for O-H (broad) and N-H (sharper, two peaks for NH<sub>2</sub>). Near 1700 look for the strong, sharp C=O, and use the other peaks to decide among ketone, aldehyde, acid, ester and amide. Triple bonds sit near 2200, C=C near 1650, and C-O in the 1000 to 1300 range. Hydrogen bonding broadens O-H, and resonance lowers C=O.

:::quiz
Q: A spectrum has a strong, sharp peak at 1715 cm-1 and no absorption above 3000 cm-1 except ordinary C-H. Which functional group is most likely present?
A: A ketone. The sharp C=O near 1715 with no O-H or N-H band rules out acids, alcohols and amides.
Q: Why is the O-H stretch of an alcohol broad while the C=O stretch is sharp?
A: Hydrogen bonding puts O-H bonds in many slightly different environments, which spreads the absorption. C=O does not hydrogen bond with itself and gives a narrow, intense peak.
Q: A compound shows a very broad band from 2500 to 3300 cm-1 and a peak at 1710 cm-1. What is it?
A: A carboxylic acid. The extremely broad O-H band overlapping the C-H region plus a carbonyl is the acid signature.
Q: Which absorbs at the higher wavenumber, an ester C=O or an amide C=O, and why?
A: The ester. Nitrogen donates by resonance into an amide carbonyl, weakening the C=O bond and lowering its wavenumber, while the ester oxygen donates less.
Q: An alcohol is oxidized to an aldehyde. What change would you expect in the IR spectrum?
A: The broad O-H band near 3350 disappears, a strong C=O appears near 1725, and two weak aldehyde C-H peaks appear near 2720 and 2820.
:::
