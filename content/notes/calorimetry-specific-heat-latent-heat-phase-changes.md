Calorimetry measures heat by watching temperature change. Two equations cover almost every question: one for heating a substance, one for changing its phase. Add conservation of energy and you can solve any mixing problem.

## Core formulas

| Situation | Formula |
|---|---|
| Temperature change | q = mc&Delta;T |
| Phase change (T constant) | q = mL |
| Heat lost = heat gained | q<sub>lost</sub> = q<sub>gained</sub> (isolated system) |
| Water specific heat | c = 4.18 J/(g K) = 1 cal/(g K) |

c is specific heat and L is latent heat of fusion or vaporization. Heat flows from the hotter object to the colder one until temperatures match.

## Heating curve

On a plot of temperature against heat added, slopes are single phases and flat sections are phase changes. On a slope use mc&Delta;T. On a flat section the temperature does not change because the heat goes into breaking intermolecular attractions, so use mL. The vaporization plateau is much longer than the fusion plateau because L<sub>v</sub> is larger than L<sub>f</sub>.

## Worked example

Find the final temperature when 100 g of water at 80 C is mixed with 100 g of water at 20 C.

- Equal masses and equal c, so the final temperature is the average, 50 C.
- For unequal masses: m<sub>1</sub>c(80 - T) = m<sub>2</sub>c(T - 20), and c cancels.
- Heat needed to melt 10 g of ice at 0 C (L<sub>f</sub> = 334 J/g): q = 10 x 334 = 3340 J, with no temperature rise.

## Common traps

- **Using mc&Delta;T during a phase change.** The temperature is flat, so use mL.
- **Mixing signs.** Set heat lost equal to heat gained with positive magnitudes.
- **Mixing units** of J and cal, or K and C. A change of 1 K equals a change of 1 C.
- **Thinking high specific heat means heating fast.** It means slow temperature change.

## One-minute recap

q = mc&Delta;T on slopes, q = mL on plateaus. Heat lost equals heat gained. Water has a high specific heat, so it resists temperature change. Vaporization needs more heat than fusion.

:::quiz
Q: Which equation applies while ice melts at 0 C?
A: q = mL, since the temperature stays constant during the phase change.
Q: Two equal masses of water at 90 C and 10 C are mixed. What is the final temperature?
A: 50 C.
Q: Which has more latent heat for water, fusion or vaporization?
A: Vaporization.
Q: A metal has a lower specific heat than water. Equal heat is added to equal masses. Which warms more?
A: The metal.
Q: Is a temperature change of 5 K larger, smaller or equal to 5 C?
A: Equal.
:::
