**Transaminases** (also called aminotransferases) are **transferases**: they move an amino group from an amino acid onto an alpha-keto acid. The usual acceptor is **alpha-ketoglutarate**, which becomes **glutamate**. The nitrogen is then removed from glutamate as ammonium and sent into the **urea cycle**, which makes urea in the liver for excretion.

## Step 1: transamination

An amino acid and alpha-ketoglutarate swap groups:

**amino acid + alpha-ketoglutarate &harr; alpha-keto acid + glutamate**

- The enzyme class is transferase (EC 2).
- The cofactor is **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**, the active form of vitamin B<sub>6</sub>.
- The reaction is freely reversible, so it also lets cells make nonessential amino acids.
- Two clinical examples: **ALT** moves the amino group from alanine, and **AST** moves it from aspartate. Both are liver enzymes that leak into blood when hepatocytes are damaged.

The carbon skeleton left behind (pyruvate, oxaloacetate, and so on) enters glycolysis, gluconeogenesis or the citric acid cycle. Amino acids that give acetyl-CoA or ketone precursors are **ketogenic**, those that give pyruvate or TCA intermediates are **glucogenic**, and leucine and lysine are purely ketogenic.

## Step 2: oxidative deamination

Glutamate is the collection point for nitrogen. **Glutamate dehydrogenase**, an oxidoreductase in liver mitochondria, removes its amino group as free **NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>** and regenerates alpha-ketoglutarate. It uses NAD<sup>+</sup> or NADP<sup>+</sup>.

## Step 3: the urea cycle

Ammonia is toxic, especially to the brain, so the liver converts it to urea. The cycle runs partly in the mitochondrial matrix and partly in the cytosol.

| Step | Location | Enzyme | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup> + CO<sub>2</sub> to carbamoyl phosphate | Mitochondria | Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I | Rate-limiting; needs N-acetylglutamate to activate; uses 2 ATP |
| Carbamoyl phosphate + ornithine to citrulline | Mitochondria | Ornithine transcarbamylase | Citrulline exits to cytosol |
| Citrulline + aspartate to argininosuccinate | Cytosol | Argininosuccinate synthetase | Second nitrogen enters from aspartate; uses ATP |
| Argininosuccinate to arginine + fumarate | Cytosol | Argininosuccinate lyase | Fumarate links to the citric acid cycle |
| Arginine to urea + ornithine | Cytosol | Arginase | Releases urea; ornithine returns to mitochondria |

The two nitrogen atoms in urea come from **ammonia** (via carbamoyl phosphate) and **aspartate**. The carbon comes from CO<sub>2</sub>.

## Moving nitrogen safely between tissues

Free ammonia is not carried in blood. Muscle sends nitrogen to the liver as **alanine** (the glucose-alanine cycle), and most other tissues send it as **glutamine**. The liver then runs the reactions above.

## Common traps

- **Calling transaminases hydrolases or ligases.** They transfer a group, so they are transferases.
- **Forgetting PLP.** Vitamin B<sub>6</sub> deficiency impairs transamination.
- **Mixing up the two carbamoyl phosphate synthetases.** CPS I (mitochondrial, urea cycle, uses ammonia) is not CPS II (cytosolic, pyrimidine synthesis, uses glutamine).
- **Placing the whole urea cycle in the cytosol.** The first two steps are mitochondrial.
- **Missing the fumarate link.** The urea cycle produces fumarate, which feeds the citric acid cycle.

## One-minute recap

Transaminases (transferases, PLP-dependent) pass amino groups from amino acids to alpha-ketoglutarate, giving glutamate. Glutamate dehydrogenase frees NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>. The liver urea cycle turns NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>, CO<sub>2</sub> and aspartate into urea, with carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I as the regulated step.

:::quiz
Q: Which enzyme class includes transaminases?
A: Transferases (EC 2), because they transfer an amino group from one molecule to another.
Q: Which cofactor do transaminases require?
A: Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), derived from vitamin B6.
Q: Which compound is the usual amino group acceptor in transamination, and what does it become?
A: Alpha-ketoglutarate accepts the amino group and becomes glutamate.
Q: Where do the two nitrogen atoms in urea come from?
A: One comes from free ammonia (through carbamoyl phosphate) and the other from aspartate.
Q: Which urea cycle byproduct links it to the citric acid cycle?
A: Fumarate, released when argininosuccinate is cleaved to arginine.
:::
