The **absolute threshold** is the minimum stimulus intensity you can detect. The **difference threshold**, also called the **just noticeable difference (JND)**, is the minimum change between two stimuli you can detect. Both are defined as the value detected 50% of the time.

## Comparison

| | Absolute threshold | Difference threshold (JND) |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | Is anything there? | Are these two different? |
| Compares | Stimulus vs no stimulus | Stimulus vs a slightly changed stimulus |
| Detected | 50% of trials | 50% of trials |
| Example | Faintest tone you can hear in a quiet room | Smallest weight increase you can feel |

## Weber's law

The JND is not a fixed amount. It is a constant fraction of the starting intensity:

**&Delta;I / I = k**

where I is the starting intensity, &Delta;I is the JND and k is the Weber fraction for that sense.

## JND example

Suppose the Weber fraction for lifted weight is 0.02 (an illustrative value).

- Starting at 100 g, the JND is 0.02 &times; 100 = 2 g. You notice 102 g.
- Starting at 500 g, the JND is 0.02 &times; 500 = 10 g. Adding 2 g goes unnoticed.

The same logic explains why one extra candle changes a dark room but not a stadium.

## Why thresholds are not fixed

Detection also depends on the observer's expectations and motivation. Signal detection theory separates sensitivity from response bias, so the 50% point shifts with context. Sensory adaptation raises effective thresholds when a stimulus stays constant.

## Common traps

- **Treating the JND as constant in absolute units.** It scales with the starting intensity.
- **Calling the absolute threshold a hard cutoff.** It is a probability point.
- **Mixing up the two thresholds.** Absolute is stimulus vs nothing; difference is stimulus vs stimulus.

## One-minute recap

- Absolute threshold: weakest detectable stimulus.
- Difference threshold (JND): smallest detectable change.
- Weber's law: JND = k &times; starting intensity.
