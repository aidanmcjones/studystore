Evolution is a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations. Five processes can change them, and each one breaks a Hardy-Weinberg assumption. The key contrast is that natural selection is nonrandom and adaptive, while genetic drift is random and can remove alleles regardless of benefit.

## The five forces

| Force | What changes frequencies | Adaptive? | Hardy-Weinberg assumption broken |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural selection | Differential reproductive success of phenotypes | Yes | No selection |
| Genetic drift | Random sampling of alleles, strongest in small populations | No | Large population |
| Gene flow | Migration of alleles between populations | No | No migration |
| Mutation | New alleles arise (slow, the raw material) | No | No mutation |
| Nonrandom mating | Changes genotype frequencies, not allele frequencies | No | Random mating |

## Natural selection

Fitness is reproductive success, meaning the number of surviving offspring, not strength. Selection acts on phenotype, but only heritable variation changes the next generation.

- **Directional:** one extreme is favored, so the mean shifts.
- **Stabilizing:** the middle is favored and variation shrinks (human birth weight).
- **Disruptive:** both extremes are favored over the middle, which can lead to speciation.
- **Sexual selection:** traits that win mates, even at a survival cost.
- **Heterozygote advantage** keeps a harmful recessive allele common (sickle cell allele and malaria).

## Genetic drift

Drift is a random change in allele frequency from chance, and it matters most in small populations. Over time it can fix one allele (frequency 1) and lose the other. Two classic cases:

- **Bottleneck effect:** a disaster cuts the population to a few survivors, whose alleles may not match the original pool.
- **Founder effect:** a few individuals start a new population, carrying only part of the source variation. Rare alleles can be common in the new group.

## Gene flow and speciation

Gene flow makes populations more similar and reduces differences. Reproductive isolation stops it, and speciation follows. Allopatric speciation is by geographic separation, and sympatric is within one area.

## Worked example

A population of 10,000 has the allele A at p = 0.6 and a at q = 0.4. A storm leaves 10 survivors who happen to carry few a alleles, and the new frequency is q = 0.1 with p = 0.9. No allele was favored by selection. This is a bottleneck, and the later genotype frequencies are q<sup>2</sup> = 0.01 aa, 2pq = 0.18 Aa, p<sup>2</sup> = 0.81 AA.

## Common traps

- **Calling drift adaptive.** It is random.
- **Thinking fitness means strength or lifespan.** It is offspring contributed.
- **Saying mutation drives rapid change.** It supplies variation but changes frequencies slowly.
- **Saying individuals evolve.** Populations do.

## One-minute recap

Selection is nonrandom and adaptive. Drift is random and strongest in small groups, with bottleneck and founder effect as special cases. Gene flow homogenizes populations. Mutation supplies new alleles. Nonrandom mating changes genotype, not allele, frequencies.

:::quiz
Q: A small group colonizes an island and a rare allele is common in their descendants. What is this called?
A: The founder effect, a type of genetic drift.
Q: Which evolutionary force is nonrandom and adaptive?
A: Natural selection.
Q: Why does genetic drift have the greatest effect in small populations?
A: Random sampling error is larger when few alleles are passed on, so chance can shift frequencies a lot.
Q: Heterozygotes survive malaria better than either homozygote. What does this do to a harmful recessive allele?
A: It keeps the allele at a stable frequency, because heterozygote advantage balances selection against homozygotes.
Q: Does nonrandom mating change allele frequencies?
A: No. It changes genotype frequencies, for example by increasing homozygotes, but not allele frequencies.
:::
